{{Stowage}} means placing cargo on board in a safe and correct way. Good stowage protects the ship, the crew and the cargo. It also makes the discharge easy at each port. The chief officer plans the stowage before the loading starts.

The {{stowage plan}} is a drawing of the ship. It shows each hold and the deck. For each space, it shows the cargo, its weight and its port of discharge. Cargo for the first port should not be under cargo for the last port. Otherwise, the crew must move other cargo first. This loses time and money.

{{Segregation}} means keeping some cargoes apart. Some cargoes can damage other cargoes. For example, wet cargo can damage dry cargo. Cargo with a strong smell can damage food. Heavy cargo must not go on top of fragile cargo. Dangerous goods follow special segregation rules in the IMDG Code.

{{Weight distribution}} is also very important. Heavy cargo usually goes low in the hold, and light cargo goes on top. The weight must be balanced between port and starboard. With more weight on one side, the ship has a {{list}}. The weight must also be balanced between the bow and the stern. The difference between the draft forward and the draft aft is the {{trim}}. The officer plans the cargo for a safe trim and no list.

At sea, the ship rolls and pitches. Cargo can move with these motions. Moving cargo can break, damage the ship or cause a dangerous list. For this reason, all cargo needs {{securing}}. {{Dunnage}} is wood or other material under and between the cargo. It protects the cargo and helps to stop movement. {{Lashing}} uses wires, chains, straps or rods to hold the cargo in place. On container ships, a {{twist lock}} at each corner connects one container to the next.

{{Deck cargo}} needs extra care. It receives wind, rain and sea water. In bad weather, waves can hit it with great force. Deck cargo needs strong lashing for the expected weather. The crew checks and tightens the lashings during the voyage. Many ships carry a Cargo Securing Manual with the approved methods for that ship.

A good stowage plan uses all these ideas together. It puts each cargo in the right place, keeps incompatible cargoes apart, and holds everything firmly. Your plan must also keep the ship balanced.
