Arriving at a port is a busy time for the deck team. Often the ship cannot go alongside at once. It waits at anchor in the {{anchorage}} outside the port. Later, a pilot comes on board, and the ship moves to its berth. This is a typical arrival at a port like Tampico.

The first part is anchoring. The forecastle team prepares the anchor and the {{windlass}}. The officer decides how much cable to pay out. The bridge reduces speed and brings the ship to the position. The forecastle reports to the bridge during the whole manoeuvre.

The second part is ballast. At anchor, the officer prepares the ship for berthing. The trim and the draft are important. The propeller and the rudder must be deep enough for good control. The ship must also be safe for the depth of water at the berth. The officer changes the {{ballast}} to get the right condition. The engine room runs the pumps, and the officer checks the drafts.

The third part is mooring. The ship ties up to the berth with ropes. Each {{mooring line}} has a name for its position. A {{head line}} goes forward from the bow. A {{stern line}} goes aft from the stern. A {{breast line}} goes out at right angles to the ship. It holds the ship close to the berth. A {{spring}} goes forward or aft along the ship's side. Springs stop the ship moving ahead or astern.

The lines pass through a {{fairlead}} at the ship's side. On board, the crew makes them fast on a {{bollard}}. On the berth, the shore team puts the eye of the line over a bollard too. Winches on deck heave the lines tight.

Some ships have a {{constant tension winch}}. This winch keeps the tension in the line at the same level. It pays out or heaves in by itself. This is useful when the tide or the draft changes. Still, the crew must check the lines often.

The bridge controls the ship with the propeller and the rudder. Tugs often help in the port. The forecastle officer and the stern officer report every line to the bridge. Mooring lines are under high tension. The crew must stay away from the line when it is under load. A broken line can hurt or kill a person.

A good arrival needs a clear plan before it starts. Each team knows its job. All reports use short, standard phrases.
