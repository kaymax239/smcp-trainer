{{Propulsion}} is the system that moves a ship through the water. For thousands of years, ships used human force or the wind. Sailors used oars and sails. A ship under {{sail}} depends on the wind. With no wind, the ship cannot move. With the wind from ahead, the voyage is slow and long.

For a long time, builders made ships of wood. In the nineteenth century, they started to use iron, and later steel. An iron {{hull}} is stronger than a wooden hull. It can be longer and carry more cargo. It also supports the weight and the vibration of heavy engines.

The {{steam engine}} changed navigation. It burns fuel, usually coal at first, to boil water. The steam moves the parts of the engine. In 1807, the steamboat Clermont of Robert Fulton started a regular service on the Hudson River in the United States. Early steamships used a {{paddle wheel}} on each side or at the stern. Later, the screw {{propeller}} replaced the paddle wheel at sea. With steam, ships did not depend on the wind. They could follow a timetable.

Next came the {{steam turbine}}. In a turbine, steam turns a wheel with many blades at high speed. In 1897, Charles Parsons showed his small ship Turbinia. It was much faster than the other ships of that time. Turbines gave large ships high speed and smooth running.

The {{internal combustion engine}} burns fuel inside its cylinders. The most common type at sea is the {{diesel engine}}. Its name comes from the engineer Rudolf Diesel. A diesel engine uses less fuel than a steam plant of the same power. It needs fewer people in the engine room, and it is ready quickly. Today, most merchant ships use diesel engines.

In {{electric propulsion}}, generators make electricity. Then electric motors turn the propellers. Cruise ships, icebreakers and some special ships use this system. It gives very good control at low speed.

On the bridge, the deck officer gives the engine orders. Each propulsion system answers these orders in a different way. Some systems change speed quickly, and others change speed slowly. For safe {{manoeuvring}}, the officer must know the system of the ship. In your report, you must explain why this knowledge matters to the deck watch.
