The bow and the stern are the two ends of the ship. In {{heavy weather}}, these parts receive strong blows from the sea. For this reason, shipbuilders make them stronger than the rest of the hull. They add extra frames, plates and beams.

At the front of the ship is the {{stem}}. The stem is the main structural piece at the bow. The side plates of the bow join it. On many modern ships, the stem is made of thick steel plate. Inside the bow, there are flat plates across the ship. Each one is a {{breasthook}}. A breasthook joins the two sides of the bow and holds them together.

The bow also has a {{collision bulkhead}}. It is a strong watertight wall near the front of the ship. If the bow is damaged, it stops water entering the rest of the ship. The space in front of it is the fore peak.

Two effects of the sea are important at the bow. The first is {{panting}}. The water pressure on the bow plates changes with every wave. The plates move in and out a little. Extra frames, beams and stringers resist this movement. The second effect is {{pounding}}. In a head sea, the ship pitches up and down. The bow rises out of the water and falls back hard. The flat bottom near the bow hits the sea surface. For this reason, the bottom structure forward is thicker and stronger.

At the back of the ship is the {{sternframe}}. It supports the rudder and the propeller area. On a single screw ship, the sternframe has an opening for the propeller. This opening is the {{propeller aperture}}. In front of the opening there is a propeller post. Behind it there is a rudder post.

On a twin screw ship, there are two propellers, one on each side. The sternframe has no propeller aperture in the middle. Each propeller shaft comes out of the hull on its own side. A {{shaft bracket}} supports each shaft near its propeller.

Many modern ships have a flat stern called a {{transom}}. The stern also receives loads. Waves from behind hit the stern. The propeller causes vibration in the structure. For this reason, the stern has strong frames and plates too.

Good reinforcement at both ends keeps the hull safe in rough seas. Still, the structure has limits. The officer on watch must watch how the ship moves in the sea.
